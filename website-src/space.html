Exoplanet transit vetting · Kepler & TESS

Somewhere in this starlight, a planet just went by.

When a planet passes in front of its star, the star gets a little dimmer. NASA's Kepler and TESS telescopes recorded that brightness for a huge number of stars. We built TransitVet to go through the data, find dips that repeat, and then do the harder part: question every dip until we can say whether it could be a planet.

Most dips aren't planets.

Two · how we check

Seven steps for every star, nine questions for every dip.

None of these steps is magic. Most are ordinary signal processing, the same tools used on radio and audio, pointed at a star instead.

  1. 1

    Get the data

    Pull the light curve from NASA's MAST archive: a point every 30 minutes for Kepler, every 2 minutes for TESS. You can also give it your own file.

  2. 2

    Clean it

    Drop the points the telescope itself marked as bad, and line up each stretch of data so they join without steps.

  3. 3

    Flatten the star

    Stars drift in brightness on their own. A biweight filter and a spline take out the slow drift so only short dips remain.

  4. 4

    Search

    Box Least Squares tries 20,000 to 150,000 possible orbits and keeps up to three dips that repeat.

  5. 5

    Fold

    Stack every orbit on top of the others. We keep two pictures: the whole orbit, and a close-up of the dip.

  6. 6

    Score

    Three models look at it: gradient boosting on measured numbers, the AstroNet CNN, and a Transformer. Their scores are joined into one.

  7. 7

    Decide

    Candidate, false alarm, or "a person should look at this". Each one comes with a report card in plain words and times to observe the next dip.

The nine questions

Every signal gets all of them. The answers go on its report card, so you can see why the tool decided what it did.

Are odd and even dips the same?

Two stars of different sizes take turns: deep dip, shallow dip. A planet makes the same dip every time.

Is there a dip half an orbit later?

A companion star that glows shows up as a second, smaller dip when it goes behind. A planet usually doesn't.

Flat bottom or V?

We fit a transit model (batman) to measure the shape. Stars just grazing each other make V-shaped dips.

Is it planet-sized?

Dip depth plus the star's size from the TESS Input Catalog. Anything over two Jupiters is almost surely a star.

Does the light stay put?

If the star's image slides across the camera during the dip, the dip may belong to a neighbour.

Which pixels went dark?

We compare the camera images in and out of the dip. If a nearby star dimmed instead, it gets named.

Does the dip fit the star?

How long a dip lasts says how dense the star should be. A big mismatch hints that a different star is involved.

What does NASA's model say?

For TESS we show the score from NASA's ExoMiner++ catalogue next to ours, when it has one.

Can we promise anything?

Keep, drop, or ask a person. Set up so that at least 95% of real planets are never dropped.
